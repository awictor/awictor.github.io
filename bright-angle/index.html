STEP 01 · ONE RAY

Follow a single ray into the drop

A raindrop is a tiny sphere of water. A ray of sunlight strikes it, bends on the way in (refraction), bounces off the far inside wall (internal reflection), then bends again on the way out. Drag the entry point up and down and watch the exit angle change.

The impact parameter is how far off-center the ray strikes — 0 through the middle, 1 grazing the rim. It sets the angle of incidence via sinθⁱ = b.

STEP 02 · SNELL & DEVIATION

Refraction sets the bending

At each surface the ray obeys Snell’s law. Air-to-water uses the refractive index n of water (about 1.33). The HUD reads out the incidence angle θⁱ, the refracted angle θₕ, and the running total deviation Δ — how far the ray got turned from a straight path.

n₁ sinθⁱ = n₂ sinθₕ  ·  Δ = 180° + 2θⁱ − 4θₕ

Nudge ↑↓ near b ≈ 0.86 and notice Δ barely changes — that stall is the whole secret.

STEP 03 · THE CAUSTIC

Many rays — and the light piles up

Now fire dozens of parallel rays across the whole drop. Because Δ has a minimum, rays near that minimum all leave in nearly the same direction. They bunch together into a bright edge — a caustic. That concentration of light is the rainbow. No light scatters past it, which is why the sky beyond looks darker.

STEP 04 · THE MINIMUM

The Descartes curve pins the angle

Plot deviation Δ against impact parameter and the story becomes a single number. The curve bottoms out at Δmin ≈ 138°, and the angle you actually see — measured from the point opposite the sun — is 180° − Δmin ≈ 42°.

cos²θⁱ = (n² − 1) / 3  →  rainbow angle ≈ 42°
STEP 05 · COLOR

Why a band of color, not a line

Water bends blue light a hair more than red — its index depends on wavelength. So each color has its own minimum-deviation angle: red near 42.4°, violet near 40.6°. Sweep the wavelength slider and paint the spectrum. The caustic smears across ~1.8° and becomes the colored band.

Colors are computed by real CIE spectral-to-sRGB conversion — toggle the colorblind-safe mapping to keep the ordering readable.

STEP 06 · THE SKY

Assemble the whole arc

Every drop at 42° from the antisolar point sends you its rainbow, so the bow is a circle. Rays that reflect twice inside build a fainter secondary bow near 51° with its colors reversed. Between them lies Alexander’s dark band — the region no scattered ray can reach. Lower the sun and the whole bow rises.

Every brightness here is a real Fresnel coefficient: the primary is bright, the secondary faint, the band dark — physics, not a painted gradient.
