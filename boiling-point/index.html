Why altitude matters in the kitchen

Water boils when its vapor pressure matches the surrounding air pressure. Higher up, the air is thinner, so water reaches that point at a lower temperature — roughly 100 °C − 0.0034 °C per metre of elevation. In Denver (~1,609 m) water boils near 94.5 °C; on a 3,000 m peak, about 90 °C. Cooler water means food cooks slower, so recipes need longer times (and pasta or eggs behave differently). Below sea level it's the opposite — water boils slightly hotter. A useful cooking approximation, not a lab-grade figure. Everything runs locally; nothing leaves your browser.
